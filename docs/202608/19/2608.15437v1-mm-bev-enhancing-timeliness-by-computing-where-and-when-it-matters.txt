# MM-BEV: Enhancing Timeliness by Computing Where and When it Matters 

Liangkai Liu ∗ and Kang G. Shin †∗Department of Computer Science, Texas Tech University, USA 

> †

Department of Computer Science and Engineering, University of Michigan, USA 

Abstract —Multi-modality Bird’s-Eye-View (MM-BEV) percep-tion, which combines LiDAR’s depth precision with cameras’ dense semantics, is essential for autonomous vehicles (AVs) but difficult to run in real time due to its high compute cost and non-ideal sensing conditions. State-of-the-art (SOTA) approaches compress single-modality detectors and fail to account for three key aspects: (1) structured intra-modality sparsity of camera and LiDAR inputs, (2) the inter-modal timing misalignment between them, and (3) most detected objects are not relevant to the planner’s immediate control action. We present MM-BEV , a real-time MM-BEV system organized around a fundamental principle: compute where and when it matters . MM-BEV decomposes perception into a mandatory part— safety-critical objects within the ego’s braking distance and short time-to-collision (TTC)—and an optional part, prioritizing the mandatory part for fast completion while down-sampling or shedding optional work if the compute budget is tight. It combines four mechanisms: (i) a criticality-ranked temporal-ROI selector driven by motion-extrapolated previous-frame detections; (ii) sparse ROI-aware feature extraction with a shared-shape camera crop at context-adaptive resolution and ROI-aware Li-DAR voxelization; (iii) a latency-aware coordinator that schedules LiDAR sweeps, image resolution, and keyframes from scene dynamics and TTC; and (iv) an asynchronous scheduler that decouples sensing from inference and skips stale frames to keep end-to-end (e2e) latency low. On the nuScenes dataset, MM-BEV reduces inference latency by 1.96 × and e2e latency by 2.93 ×, preserving geometry-critical 

recall exactly and dropping safety-critical recall by only 0.2 pp. On a Clearpath Husky A300 robot with an Ouster-128 LiDAR, BEV cameras, and a Jetson AGX Orin, MM-BEV achieves a further 2.11 × mean-latency reduction, corroborating its potential for real-world autonomy. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

An autonomous vehicle (AV) must perceive its surroundings in real time—planner cannot use late sensor frames. Bird’s-Eye-View (BEV) perception has become the dominant rep-resentation for this task: by projecting onboard camera and LiDAR data into a unified 360° top-down view [1], [2], it blends LiDAR’s precise depth with the cameras’ dense seman-tic cues for robust detection across diverse conditions [3], [4]. However, on a resource-limited platform aboard AVs, accuracy and the real-time constraint pull against each other: fusing two high-rate, high-resolution sensor streams is expensive, and multi-modality BEV perception is notoriously hard to run in real time [3], [5]–[10]. Prior work has approached this real-time/latency challenge along three axes, each of which captures only a slice of the problem. The standard solution is to make the detector cheaper—compressing dense models into sparse ones [6], [11]–[14]—but this still treats every region of the input uniformly. Region-of-Interest (ROI) heuristics come closer, yet have been explored almost entirely on camera images and within a single modality [15], [16]; they never allocate compute across camera and LiDAR explicitly. On the timing axis, most pipelines assume pre-synchronized inputs or absorb worst-case delays without exploiting the detection task’s struc-ture [17], [18]. The two efforts closest to ours each capture only one slice: RT-BEV [9] couples ROI-based detection with synchronization but is camera-only and ignores cross-modal redundancy and asynchronous fusion; FLEX [19] adapts the LiDAR sweep count per view under a global time budget, but at coarse per-view granularity and without exploiting frame-to-frame temporal coherence. Moreover, every one of these systems is judged by all-object accuracy—the metric that mislabels a planning-safe speedup as an accuracy loss. To understand where on-board compute is actually redun-dant, we profiled the multi-modality BEV pipeline (§III). The inefficiency is structured , with the two modalities incurring redundant work in complementary dimensions. Cameras are 

spatially sparse : only a small, stable fraction of image regions ever contains an object, yet a dense backbone pays for ev-ery pixel. LiDAR is temporally redundant : aggregating more sweeps yields quickly diminishing the accuracy gain while latency grows steadily. Compounding both, the sensors are 

asynchronous —running at different rates with non-negligible communication delays, so their timestamps are rarely aligned and the inter-modal offsets silently degrade fusion [20], [21]. These findings frame our research problem. Existing BEV perception systems ignore this structure—they spend compute uniformly across input they could route, and across sweeps they could shed—and they are evaluated by a metric that hides whether the spared compute would have mattered for the planner. For a real-time safety system, the metric is wrong: as in imprecise computation , the perception task has a mandatory part—the safety-critical objects within the ego’s braking distance and short time-to-collision (TTC), which the planner must act on within TTC—and an optional part. The right question is, therefore, not “how fast can the model be,” but which work is mandatory, and how do we prioritize it for fast completion? 

We answer this questionby developing MM-BEV , a real-time multi-modality BEV perception system organized around a fundamental principle: compute where and when it mat-arXiv:2608.15437v1 [cs.RO] 15 Aug 2026 ters . MM-BEV routes both modalities’ compute by the pre-vious frame’s motion-extrapolated detections ( temporal-ROI 

routing), and co-designs per-frame workload reduction with pipeline scheduling so that the mandatory detections are always processed first and with the lowest possible latency. 

MM-BEV consists of four parts. (i) A criticality-ranked temporal-ROI selector projects the previous frame’s motion-padded detections into per-modality ROIs and ranks them by geometry and safety criticality, so the mandatory set is always covered. (ii) Sparse ROI-aware feature extraction confines the camera backbone to a shared-shape ROI crop at a context-adaptive resolution, and confines LiDAR voxelization to the ROI point set. (iii) A latency-aware coordinator holds the per-frame budget by scheduling the LiDAR sweep window, image resolution, and periodic full-scene keyframes from scene dynamics and TTC. (iv) An asynchronous streaming scheduler decouples sensing from inference and processes only the freshest synchronized sensor data, so the pipeline stays responsive instead of falling behind on a growing queue. We implement MM-BEV and evaluate it e2e in ROS on the nuScenes dataset [3], [22], [23] on a single NVIDIA RTX 3080 GPU. Temporal-ROI routing with adaptive resolu-tion cuts mean inference latency 1.96 × (202 → 103 ms) for the LiDAR+camera detector and 1.93 × (136 → 70 ms) for the LiDAR-only detector; the asynchronous scheduler cuts e2e la-tency 2.93 × (1006 → 344 ms) and raises processed frames per scene from ∼10 to 60 . Most importantly, this does not cost the mandatory work: geometry-critical recall is preserved exactly 

and safety-critical recall drops only 0.2 points. A deployment on a Clearpath Husky A300 with a Jetson AGX Orin reaches a further 2.11 × mean-latency reduction, confirming the gains transfer to real-world autonomous systems. This paper makes four contributions:

State-of-the-art (SOTA) Bird’s-Eye View (BEV) perception typically relies on indiscriminate processing of large-scale input data from both LiDARs and cameras [2], [3], [5]–[7], [12], [24]. Fig. 1 present a detailed BEV perception pipeline with both LiDAR and surrounding cameras. The pipeline usually consists of two critical stages: communication and detec-tion [9]. The communication stage involves two subprocesses: capturing and synchronization. In the former, multiple cameras (typically including front, front-right, front-left, back, back-left, and back-right) and LiDAR sensors independently acquire environmental data [22], [25]–[28]. The latter (synchroniza-tion) ensures temporal alignment across these data streams by matching timestamps associated with each sensor’s data, maintaining the coherence required for precise perception. Once synchronized, the detection stage begins by processing the sensor data via two parallel branches—one for camera-based images and another for LiDAR point clouds. Multiple lidar sweeps are usually accumulated for a richer point cloud as input [22]. Each branch independently extracts meaningful spatial features from its respective sensor modality. These separate features are then converted into a unified BEV rep-resentation, creating aligned BEV feature maps. A detection head is then employed on these fused BEV features to effec-tively detect and localize relevant objects in the environment, ultimately outputting accurate and timely detection results essential for AV operation. III. E MPIRICAL STUDY 

We derive MM-BEV ’s design principles from empirical stud-ies of the SOTA BEV perception pipeline with LiDAR and camera sensors. We identify three key insights for accuracy and real-time capability in BEV perception with LiDAR and cameras: 1) fine-grained ROIs are necessary; 2) asynchronous sensor timing leads to misalignment; and 3) most detected objects are safety-noncritical, so accuracy must be measured against the safety-relevant subset.

B. Why Co-Optimize Sync and Detection? 

Asynchronous sensor timing is the norm, not an exception. 

Most BEV perception pipelines assume perfectly synchronized LiDAR and camera inputs [22], [25], yet real systems run sensors on separate clocks and at different rates, introducing unavoidable temporal offsets [9], [20], [30]. In nuScenes, LiDAR samples at 20 Hz while the surround cameras run at 12 Hz; the official dataset exposes only 2 Hz keyframes 

for training/evaluation. Even when an ApproximateTime 

policy in message_filters is used for synchronization, residual offsets on the order of 50 –125 ms are common due to sampling-rate mismatch and bus jitter. When multi-sweep LiDAR aggregation is enabled to densify the point cloud, the effective sensing window can span up to ∼ 500 ms (keyframe at 2 Hz + several preceding non-keyframe sweeps), further widening inter-modal misalignment.

IV. S YSTEM DESIGN 

This section details the design of MM-BEV . Building on the three insights of Section III, MM-BEV routes both modal-ities’ compute through fine-grained ROIs (addressing the spa-tial/temporal redundancy of Insight 1), treats synchronization and detection as a joint problem to mitigate the inter-modal timing offsets we measured (Insight 2), and decomposes per-ception into a mandatory part — the safety-critical detections 4a planner must act on this cycle — and an optional part (Insight 3), prioritizing the mandatory part for low-latency completion and shedding optional work when the compute budget is tight. We first give an overview, then state the technical challenges, then detail each component.

Fig. 7 depicts the architecture of MM-BEV . Unlike the conventional pipeline of Fig. 1, which densely extracts features from every frame, MM-BEV routes compute by where it matters and when it matters . It begins with a criticality-ranked temporal-ROI selector 1 : rather than re-scanning each frame, it projects the previous frame’s motion-extrapolated detections into per-modality regions of interest (ROIs) and ranks them by criticality, so the mandatory set is always covered while redundant coverage is dropped. A synchronization module 2assembles the raw sensor data for the selected ROIs from a buffer and motion-compensates multi-sweep LiDAR to a common reference time. The camera branch performs sparse ROI-aware feature extraction 3 , running the backbone only on a shared-shape ROI crop at a context-adaptive resolution; the LiDAR branch uses ROI-aware voxelization 4 , voxeliz-ing only the ROI point set. At the core, a latency-aware coordinator 5 holds the per-frame budget — scheduling the LiDAR sweep window, the image resolution, and periodic full-scene keyframes from scene dynamics and time-to-collision (TTC) — and runs the whole pipeline under an asynchronous producer/consumer scheduler so the model always consumes the freshest synchronized sensor bundle. 

B. Technical Challenges 

MM-BEV is designed to address four challenges in achieving efficient and accurate BEV perception with real-time latency. 

C1: Modeling where compute matters. How can the scene be modeled to route compute to the regions that matter, without missing safety-critical objects? MM-BEV exploits temporal coherence — the previous frame’s detections, constrained by physical motion, localize this frame’s objects — and ranks the resulting ROIs by criticality derived from ego state, so the mandatory set is never dropped. 

C2: Mitigating sensor misalignment. How can tempo-ral misalignment across asynchronous sensors be mini-mized? MM-BEV forms ROI-guided synchronization groups and motion-compensates multi-sweep LiDAR to a common reference time before fusion. 

C3: Processing sparse, variable-sized inputs. How can feature extraction handle sparse ROIs efficiently on encoders built for dense inputs? MM-BEV runs the camera backbone on a shared-shape ROI crop at a context-adaptive resolution and confines LiDAR voxelization to the ROI point set — limiting computation to informative regions without padding waste. 

C4: Keeping latency low. How can the latency–accuracy trade-off be managed so the pipeline sustains real-time rates? 

A latency-aware coordinator schedules keyframes, the LiDAR sweep window, and image resolution from scene dynamics and TTC, and an asynchronous scheduler keeps end-to-end latency low by always running on the freshest sensor bundle.

E. Sparse ROI-Aware Camera Feature Extraction 

Image ROIs occupy only a small fraction of the dense image, so the camera backbone should not process every pixel. 

Shared-shape ROI crop. MM-BEV crops each camera to its ROI window and normalizes all crops to a single shared shape 

(target h, target w), sized from the largest projected ROI and snapped to the backbone stride. Per-camera offsets place each crop; cameras with no ROI get a small center crop. Because all crops share a shape, the backbone forward is a single batched pass with no padding waste. After the backbone and neck, the cropped features are pasted onto a full-image feature canvas at the correct stride, and a binary ROI mask marks the pasted region. The mask is carried into the view transform and zeroes the BEV contribution of canvas regions that held no real features, removing the camera-side accuracy floor that a zero-padded canvas would otherwise introduce. 

Context-adaptive resolution. The camera backbone is the dominant cost in the image branch. MM-BEV runs it at a context-adaptive resolution: when the scene is slow and sparse, the ROI crop is downsampled before the backbone and the features are upsampled back, halving the image backbone cost; when the scene is fast and dense, full resolution is kept. The resolution choice is made per frame by the coordinator (§IV-G).

G. Latency-Aware Coordinator 

The coordinator holds the per-frame latency budget. It does so along two axes: compute scaling — adjusting how much work each frame does — and pipeline scheduling — ensuring the model always runs on fresh data. 

Time-to-collision. The coordinator’s signals come from ego/object motion. Given an obstacle’s relative position p =[x1, y 1] and velocity v = [ vx − vego , v y ], the time of closest approach is 

t∗ = − p · v

∥v∥2 , (8) and TTC = t∗ if t∗ > 0 and ∥p + t∗v∥ ≤ Rcoll , else TTC = 

∞. The minimum TTC over the scene drives both the safety-criticality test (§IV-C) and the coordinator’s budget decisions. 

Priority-based budget management. The coordinator has two compute knobs—the LiDAR sweep count n and the image 

resolution —and applies them in priority order. Mandatory-ROI coverage is fixed first and cannot be shed. The sweep count is reduced next, from n=10 at high ego speed down to n=5 

when the ego is slow or the scene is dense (recent frames already carry the needed context). Image resolution is dropped last, to half when the scene is slow and sparse and kept full otherwise. If the minimum configuration still risks running long, the coordinator falls back to LiDAR-only inference for that frame, preserving the mandatory LiDAR-ROI path while shedding the camera branch entirely. Both knobs trade optional fidelity for latency and never touch the mandatory ROIs; on the evaluation platform, the half-resolution n=5 ROI path consistently fits within the ∼100 ms per-frame target. 

Keyframes. ROI-based perception risks error accumulation and stale context, since newly appearing objects have no tem-poral precedent. MM-BEV periodically inserts a keyframe — a full-scene pass that refreshes the detection cache and bounds the worst-case staleness of the temporal prior. ROS framework. To emulate real-time streaming process-ing similarly to actual AVs, we implement MM-BEV using ROS [23], [32], as depicted in Fig. 11. Specifically, we develop three ROS nodes: Sensor Publisher , Synchronizer ,and Detector . The Sensor Publisher publishes ROS bags generated from nuScenes dataset, incorporating raw sensor data (6 cameras and 1 LiDAR) alongside CAN bus information from the ego vehicle. Camera frames are pub-lished at 12 Hz, while LiDAR data is published at 20 Hz. The 

Synchronizer node consists of a Sweep Buffer that caches previous LiDAR sweeps, a Transform module for precomputing metadata containing sensor coordinate transfor-mations (rotation and translation), and a Sync Callback ,which serves as the entry point for synchronizing sensor data based on the ROIs within a configurable slop threshold. The Detector node closely follows the design structure of MM-BEV , with minor adaptations for ROS-based inter-module communication using ROS parameters. Specifically, parameters like slop and sync_group are employed by the Coordinator to dynamically adjust synchronization set-tings. The keyframe parameter also indicates the keyframe mode, and ROI C and ROI L parameters are used for real-time updates of ROIs for cameras and LiDAR, respectively.

VII. R ELATED WORK 

BEV perception has become mainstream for AVs, thanks to its comprehensive view understanding, which guides their planning and navigation [1], [2]. Compared to vision-centric BEV perception that utilizes only camera inputs [8], [36], [37], multi-modality BEV perception combining LiDAR and cameras is more practical and widely deployed, primarily due to its superior accuracy and reliability across diverse traffic scenarios [3], [24]. However, achieving real-time BEV perception remains chal-lenging due mainly to the high computational demands from high-frequency and high-resolution multi-modality sensors [3]. SOTA research has primarily approached this challenge by either designing lightweight models or compressing dense models into sparse structures to improve computational ef-ficiency [5], [6], [12]. For instance, BEVFusion [3] utilizes dense camera and LiDAR feature maps to perform sensor fusion within a shared BEV space. Transfusion [5] maintains dense features for the camera branch but uses sparse projection for LiDAR inputs. SparseFusion [12] employs sparse features and fusion to reduce overhead. Cross-Modality Transformer (CMT) [6] further explores efficient multi-modal integration by designing a transformer-based feature transformation. Yet, 0123

Remix and Elf recognize the variable importance of regions and but only apply to one camera, not applied directly to multi-modality sensors [15], [16]. FLEX considers BEV cameras but only focuses on frame-level ROIs [19]. RT-BEV [9] tackles the ROI and synchronization issues of cameras only. We presented MM-BEV , a real-time multi-modality BEV perception system organized around the principle of computing 

where and when it matters . MM-BEV decomposes BEV detec-tion into a mandatory part—the critical objects inside the ego’s braking distance and short time-to-collision—and an optional 

part, and combines four mechanisms (a criticality-ranked temporal-ROI selector, sparse ROI-aware feature extraction, a latency-aware coordinator, and an asynchronous streaming scheduler). On nuScenes—where only 11% of annotated ob-jects are mandatory under the criticality model— MM-BEV 

cuts inference latency by 1.96 × and end-to-end latency by 

2.93 ×, preserves geometry-critical recall exactly, and drops safety-critical recall by only 0.2 percentage points. On a Clearpath Husky A300 robot with Jetson AGX Orin, MM-BEV 

reaches a further 2.11 × mean-latency reduction under an adaptive keyframe schedule, confirming that the gains transfer to embedded hardware.
