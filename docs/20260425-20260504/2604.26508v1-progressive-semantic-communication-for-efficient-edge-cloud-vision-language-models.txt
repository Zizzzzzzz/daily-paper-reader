# Progressive Semantic Communication for Efficient Edge-Cloud Vision-Language Models 

Cyril Shih-Huan Hsu ∗ Wig Yuan-Cheng Cheng † Chrysa Papagianni ∗

Abstract 

Deploying Vision–Language Models (VLMs) on edge devices remains challenging due to their substantial compu-tational and memory demands, which exceed the capabilities of resource-constrained embedded platforms. Conversely, fully offloading inference to the cloud is often impractical in bandwidth-limited environments, where transmitting raw visual data introduces substantial latency overhead. While recent edge–cloud collaborative architectures attempt to partition VLM workloads across devices, they typically rely on transmitting fixed-size representations, lacking adaptability to dynamic network conditions and failing to fully exploit semantic redundancy. In this paper, we propose a progressive semantic communication framework for edge–cloud VLM inference, using a Meta AutoEncoder that compresses visual tokens into adaptive, progressively refinable representations, enabling plug-and-play deployment with off-the-shelf VLMs without additional fine-tuning. This design allows flexible transmission at different informa-tion levels, providing a controllable trade-off between communication cost and semantic fidelity. We implement a full end-to-end edge–cloud system comprising an embedded NXP i.MX95 platform and a GPU server, communicating over bandwidth-constrained networks. Experimental results show that, at 1 Mbps uplink, the proposed progressive scheme significantly reduces network latency compared to full-edge and full-cloud solutions, while maintaining high semantic consistency even under high compression. The implementation code will be released upon publication at https://github.com/open-ep/ProSemComVLM.

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Vision-language models (VLMs) have rapidly emerged as a cornerstone of multimodal artificial intelligence, enabling machines to jointly reason over visual and textual inputs for tasks such as image captioning, visual question answering, and multimodal interaction. Despite their strong performance, the deployment of VLMs in real-world systems remains challenging due to their substantial computational and memory requirements. To address these challenges, recent research has explored collaborative edge–cloud (or edge-to-server) inference paradigms, which leverage the modular structure of VLMs to distribute computation across devices. In such frameworks, lightweight processing, such as visual encoding or preliminary inference, is performed at the edge, while more computationally intensive language reasoning is delegated to centralized servers. Beyond simple partitioning, more advanced approaches further optimize communication efficiency by transmitting only task-relevant information or adaptively requesting additional data based on model uncertainty, thereby balancing accuracy, latency, and bandwidth usage. These developments highlight a growing trend toward semantic-aware and resource-efficient VLM deployment, where the goal is not only to distribute computation, but also to minimize unnecessary data transmission while preserving task performance. However, existing edge–cloud VLM systems still face several key limitations. First, most approaches rely on transmitting either raw pixels or fixed-size latent representations, which are inherently non-adaptive and fail to account for dynamic network conditions or task-dependent semantic requirements (e.g., [1]). Second, while some methods incorporate semantic-aware or uncertainty-driven transmission strategies (e.g., [2], [3]), they typically adopt non-progressive mechanisms, where additional information is transmitted in coarse steps without reusing previously transmitted content. This results in inefficient communication under iterative transmission and lacks a principled way to achieve fine-grained control over the trade-off between communication cost and semantic fidelity. These limitations highlight the need for a more flexible framework that enables adaptive, progressive, and semantically meaningful transmission for edge–cloud VLM inference. In this paper, we propose a progressive semantic communication framework for efficient edge–cloud VLM deployment, with the following contributions: 1. We introduce a progressively refinable latent representation via a plug-and-play Meta AutoEncoder (MetaAE), which enables incremental transmission and reuse of partial visual semantics, thereby significantly improving flexibility and communication efficiency compared to conventional non-progressive schemes. 2. We design a semantic quality-aware transmission control that adaptively determines whether additional informa-tion should be transmitted, enabling a controllable trade-off between communication cost and task performance. 3. We implement a real-world end-to-end edge–cloud testbed on an embedded NXP i.MX95 platform and a GPU server, demonstrating the practical feasibility of the proposed framework under bandwidth-constrained conditions, and validating its effectiveness in reducing latency while preserving semantic consistency. The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Section II provides context through related work. Section III introduces the proposed framework. We then detail our experimental setup in Section IV and evaluate the framework in Section V. We conclude this work in Section VI. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Vision Language Models 

VLMs have progressed toward general-purpose multimodal reasoning by coupling pretrained vision encoders with large language models and improving cross-modal alignment and instruction following. Early modular designs such as Flamingo [4] show that bridging powerful vision-only and language-only backbones enables flexible few-shot prompting, while BLIP-2 [5] highlights a complementary efficiency point: keeping large backbones frozen and training a lightweight connector can yield strong VL capabilities with fewer trainable parameters. Instruction-tuned models such as LLaVA [6] further operationalize VLMs as conversational assistants by training on instruction-following multimodal data, shifting research emphasis from narrow benchmarks to interactive usage patterns that often demand low latency and robust deployment. More recent studies (e.g., VILA [7]) systematically analyze pretraining and data mixture choices that materially affect both performance and engineering cost, while large technical reports such as Qwen3-VL [8] reflect scaling trends that intensify resource pressures and motivate deployment optimization. 

B. Semantic Communication 

Semantic communication research reframes transmission objectives from reconstructing raw signals to preserving task-relevant information, increasingly using learned representations aligned to downstream goals. Transformer-based semantic image transmission (WITT [9]) demonstrates end-to-end learned coding optimized for channel conditions, Preprint. Under review. April 2026

Token-based paradigms (TokCom [11]) further align with modern VLM/LLM interfaces by treating tokens as the unit of communication and exploiting cross-modal context during reconstruction or generation. VLF-MSC [12] proposes sending a compact shared vision-language feature that conditions both language generation and image generation at the receiver. Video TokenCom [13] extends this direction by allocating rate/protection based on textual intent and token importance, which suggests principled ways to couple application goals with source–channel adaptation. 

C. Edge-Cloud Deployment 

Edge–cloud deployment work addresses the systems bottlenecks that arise when VLMs must run under tight latency, energy, and bandwidth constraints. Empirical evaluation on smartphones shows that even mobile-oriented VLM stacks can remain CPU-bound and underutilize accelerators, motivating careful runtime and partitioning design [14]. At the architectural level, Distributed VLMs [1] exploit the modularity of VLMs by offloading visual encoding to edge devices while retaining language generation on servers, improving throughput via parallelization. Collaborative strategies can further reduce uplink overhead by transmitting only task-relevant information. For example, [2] uses VLM attention for patch selection and an entropy-based uncertainty trigger for selective re-transmission. Similarly, [3] introduces a region-of-interest generation mechanism to reduce bandwidth consumption during transmission. These approaches align with the principle of semantic-aware communication in edge–cloud inference loops. III. M ETHODOLOGY 

A. System Overview 

We propose an edge–cloud collaborative framework for pregressive semantic communication with VLMs, as illustrated in Fig. 2. The system is designed to minimize communication overhead while preserving task-relevant semantic information through progressive and adaptive transmission over a practical RESTful interface. At the 

edge side , an input image is first processed by a lightweight vision encoder, producing high-dimensional latent representations. These latents are further compressed by a meta encoder into a progressively refinable representation. This representation is explicitly ordered, such that earlier segments capture coarse semantic information, while subsequent segments provide incremental refinements that progressively improve the reconstruction quality. A transmission control module (CTRL in Fig. 2) selects an initial subset (e.g., 40 %) of the representation based on communication constraints. The selected segments are serialized into a compact JSON payload and transmitted to the cloud via an HTTP POST request. At the cloud side , the received payload is parsed and passed through a meta decoder to reconstruct the latent representation. A semantic quality estimator then evaluates whether the reconstructed latent satisfies a predefined quality threshold ϵ for the downstream task. If the estimated quality is sufficient, the reconstructed latent is combined with the textual input and forwarded to the LLM decoder to generate Preprint. Under review. April 2026 the final response. Otherwise, the system enters a closed-loop refinement process: the cloud sends a feedback signal via an HTTP response requesting additional representation segments from the edge. The edge then transmits further incremental information, which is accumulated at the cloud to progressively improve the reconstruction. This process repeats until the target semantic quality is achieved (see Sec. III-C).

C. Meta AutoEncoder 

To realize the progressively refinable representation, we design a Meta AutoEncoder with three components: ( i) an encoder that maps the original latent into ordered transmission chunks, ( ii ) a prefix masking module that simulates partial transmission, and ( iii ) a decoder that reconstructs the latent while estimating its quality. We implement both encoder and decoder using Transformer encoder layers [15]. As a result, the representation is naturally structured as a sequence of tokens, where each token corresponds to a transmission unit. In the following, we use the term 

token instead of chunk for clarity and consistency with transformer architectures. 

Meta Encoder. Given the visual latent z ∈ Rd, the encoder produces an ordered representation as defined in Eq. (1) and (2), where each token is arranged according to transmission priority. Earlier tokens capture coarse semantic information, while later token provide finer refinements. 

Prefix Masking Module. To simulate progressive transmission during training, a transmission level ℓ ∈ { 1, . . . , K }

is sampled uniformly at random. Only the prefix r(ℓ) = r1, r 2, . . . , r ℓ

 is kept, while the remaining tokens are replaced with zero padding tokens . This results in a masked representation ˜r(ℓ), which serves as input to the decoder. While uniform sampling provides a simple and unbiased training strategy, exploring optimal sampling distributions for different deployment scenarios is left for future work. The theoretical justification for how prefix masking encourages progressively refinable representations is provided in Appendix A. D. Semantic Quality-Aware Transmission Control 

Based on the Meta AutoEncoder, we define a semantic quality metric to guide adaptive transmission. In Eq. (7), the decoder predicts a reconstruction error ˆe(ℓ) for a given transmission level ℓ. We map this value to a bounded quality score as 

q(ℓ) = exp   − ˆe(ℓ), q(ℓ) ∈ [0 , 1] , (12) where higher values indicate better reconstruction quality. This formulation ensures a monotonic relationship between reconstruction error and semantic quality, while normalizing the scale for consistent threshold-based decision making. 

Semantic-Aware Quality Metric. The proposed quality metric is inherently semantic-aware . Specifically, the reconstruction target z is the latent representation extracted from a pretrained vision encoder with frozen weights. As such, z encodes high-level semantic information rather than low-level pixel details. Minimizing the reconstruction error in this latent space is therefore analogous to perceptual loss [17], capturing semantic fidelity. We further validate this design in Sec. V, where we show that the proposed semantic quality q(ℓ) is highly correlated with downstream task performance (e.g., caption similarity), demonstrating that it serves as an effective surrogate for task-level performance in transmission control. 

Adaptive Transmission Control. The semantic quality score is used to drive a closed-loop transmission process between the edge and the cloud. Given a predefined quality threshold ϵ ∈ [0 , 1] , the cloud evaluates q(ℓ) after each transmission step and determines whether additional information is required. Specifically, starting from an initial transmission level ℓ0, the system iteratively performs: 1. Edge → Cloud: transmit the next-level token; 2. Cloud: reconstruct ˆz(ℓ) and compute q(ℓ);3. Decision: if q(ℓ) ≥ ϵ or ℓ = K, terminate; otherwise, request further transmission and return to Step 1. This process forms a feedback-driven control loop, enabling the system to adaptively balance communication cost and semantic fidelity. Fig. 4 illustrates the corresponding sequential diagram. In practice, the encoding process is performed only once at the initial iteration to generate the full ordered token sequence. The resulting tokens corresponding to all transmission levels are stored at the edge-side controller, and subsequent iterations simply transmit additional tokens from this cached representation without recomputing the encoder. The NPU supports hardware-accelerated execution of both CNN and transformer-based architectures. The platform runs a Yocto Project-based Linux distribution with kernel version Linux kernel 6.12. B. Evaluation Metrics 

Due to the limited capacity of the SmolVLM-256M model, absolute task accuracy is not the main focus of our evaluation. Instead, we assess how well the proposed method preserves the Semantic Consistency (SC) of the original model outputs under progressive transmission. In other words, the original model output is treated as the reference, and we measure the deviation introduced by the proposed framework. We evaluate the method on two well-established benchmarks for VLMs:  

> •

COCO Captioning (MS COCO 2017) [19]  

> •

POPE (Polling-based Object Probing Evaluation) [20] For each dataset, we randomly sample 1, 000 instances from the test split, and conduct 10 independent runs to account for stochasticity in generation. All reported results correspond to the average over these runs. Moreover, SC is quantified using a sentence embedding tool from fastembed 4. Specifically, we compute the cosine similarity 

between the embedding of answers generated by the proposed method and by the original model. This metric captures the degree to which the semantic meaning is preserved, even if the exact wording differs. In addition, we compare the following 3 deployment configurations: ( i) Full Edge , where the entire VLM is executed on the edge; ( ii ) Full Cloud , where all computation is done on a cloud server; and ( iii ) Edge-Cloud ,corresponding to the proposed progressive transmission framework that partitions computation between edge and cloud. For a fair comparison, we do not apply any entropy-based compression to the transmitted features (or pixels) in all above configurations. Fig. 5a illustrates the trade-off between Semantic Consistency (SC) and transmission delay over different LTL ratios, with SC and delay plotted on the left and right y-axes, respectively. Starting from 100 % LTL ratio, both benchmarks achieve their highest SC. As the LTL ratio gradually reduces, SC degrades gracefully, remaining above 

80 % even at aggressive compression levels (e.g., 25 %), while the transmission delay decreases almost linearly. Notably, at 25 % LTL ratio, the proposed method achieves a substantial reduction in delay ( 75 %), while incurring less than 5% degradation in SC. Additionally, Fig. 5b shows a near-perfect monotonic relationship between the proposed quality metric defined in Eq. (12) and COCO task SC, with a Spearman’s Rank Correlation Coefficient (SRCC) of 1.0. This indicates that the proposed quality metric serves as a reliable proxy for semantic accuracy, making it well-suited for guiding adaptive transmission decisions. Table II shows qualitative results of image descriptions under different LTL ratios. For this representative real-world example, no noticeable semantic deviations are observed across levels: the main scene content is described correctly in all cases, while the wording varies with the LTL ratio. The image was taken by the authors and is therefore not included in the training/validation set, which further suggests robust generalization to Out-Of-Distribution (OOD), real-world data. As shown in Table III, the Full Edge configuration is computation-bound, while the Full Cloud setting is dominated by network delay. In contrast, the proposed Edge-Cloud approach achieves the lowest end-to-end delay by balancing computation and communication. Notably, the overhead of MetaAE is marginal in both delay and energy cost ( 0.35 %), while contributing to a sharp reduction in transmission delay. VI. C ONCLUSION

We now provide a theoretical justification for why the proposed prefix-masked training strategy encourages progressively refinable latent representations. Although the coarse-to-fine structure is not imposed by explicit supervision, prefix masking introduces a structured optimization bias over the ordered token sequence. Specifically, earlier tokens are visible under more transmission levels and therefore participate in more reconstruction objectives than later tokens. Proposition 2 formalizes this asymmetric optimization pressure and shows that prefix masking naturally prioritizes earlier tokens during training. Beyond this optimization bias, progressive refinement is also supported by the nested structure of prefix transmis-sion. A higher transmission level contains all tokens available at lower levels, together with additional refinement tokens. Therefore, under sufficient decoder capacity, the best achievable reconstruction error cannot increase as the prefix length grows. Proposition 3 formalizes this nested-prefix argument and establishes that the optimal reconstruction risk is non-increasing with the transmission level. Together, these results explain why prefix-masked training encourages early tokens to encode broadly useful coarse information, while later tokens provide incremental refinements.

C. Edge–Cloud Configuration at 1 Mbps 

In the proposed Edge–Cloud configuration, the Neutron NPU executes vision_encoder_q4.onnx on the board and emits a latent tensor of shape (64 , 576) in float32 , serialized as a 192.3-KiB JSON payload. The server receives the latent, performs the LLM decode, and returns the text response. The full progressive transmission pipeline with the Meta AutoEncoder sits between the vision encoder and the HTTP layer; the measurements reported here correspond to transmission at 100% latent transmission level (LTL ratio). Fig. 7 shows the measured on-board power profile during an Edge–Cloud cycle. Excluding Run 5 (3578.8 ms, attributable to transient OS scheduling interference. See Table VI), NPU encoding is tightly bounded: the remaining nine runs have a mean of 3171 ms and a standard deviation of only ±11 ms (CV = 0.3%). Network transfer is essentially deterministic (CV = 0.03%), which is expected because the 192.3-KiB latent payload plus TBF shaping yields a repeatable 1.62-s upload. The end-to-end composition is therefore 46.3% on-board NPU encode, 23.3% network, 7.0% cloud LLM decode, with the remainder attributable to protocol and scheduling overhead. Peak RSS is 343.7 MB, of which 254.6 MB is the vision-encoder ONNX session.
